The promise

What $2,000 buys

The price holds because one operator owns the housing, the utilities and the land, and charges close to cost on each. A resident pays one monthly bill. An adult needs roughly $29,000 a year before tax to live here comfortably.

Monthly cost per adultUSD

Housing is the town-wide average rent per home (–) divided across adults. A studio is about $850, a one-bedroom $1,050, a two-bedroom $1,400, a three-bedroom $1,750, and a co-living room $600. Children ride free on utilities, transit, school and community fees. A family of two adults and two children comes to about $4,850 a month, or $1,210 per person.

Site

Why the Uncompahgre Valley

The recommended site is irrigated farmland between Montrose and Delta, Colorado. It is one of the few places in the mountain West that pairs world-class public land with flat buildable ground, senior irrigation water, strong sun, a regional hospital and a commercial airport, at farmland prices instead of resort prices.

What mattersWhat this site offers
Outdoor accessGunnison Gorge and Black Canyon of the Gunnison about 20 minutes away. Uncompahgre Plateau and Grand Mesa under an hour. Ouray ice climbing and hot springs about 50 minutes, Telluride skiing about 90 minutes, Moab about 2.5 hours.
Elevation and climateAbout 5,600 ft. Four seasons, low humidity, roughly 240 sunny days, mild valley winters with snow on the peaks in view.
WaterFarmland here carries shares in a federal irrigation project fed by the Gunnison River. Buying land with its water is the starting point for a municipal supply.
EnergyHigh-desert solar resource, about 1,750 kWh per installed kW per year, and a local electric cooperative to interconnect with.
Access and servicesMontrose Regional Airport and a regional hospital within about 15 miles. US 50 and US 550 pass the site.

Alternates worth carrying into diligence: the Cumberland Plateau near Chattanooga, Tennessee (abundant water, lower building costs, lower elevation) and the Spokane to Coeur d'Alene corridor (cheap hydro power, lakes and ski hills). How it runs

Water, power, food and daily life

Clean water

1.1 million gal/day · 1,260 acre-ft/yr
  • Supply from senior irrigation shares bought with the farmland and converted to municipal use.
  • A 2 million gal/day membrane plant, and a reservoir holding 90 days of demand.
  • Homes use 45 gallons per person per day, about half the US average, through efficient fixtures and recycled water for toilets and landscape.
  • All wastewater is treated and polished in riverside wetlands, then reused on the farm.

Clean power and air

65 MW solar · 180 MWh storage · 110 GWh/yr
  • 25 MW on roofs and 40 MW over pasture supply as much energy as the town uses in a year. The grid tie covers winter nights.
  • Every building is all-electric with heat pumps on a shared ground loop. Nothing burns in town.
  • Filtered fresh-air ventilation in every home keeps indoor air clean on wildfire smoke days.
  • Batteries keep clinics, water and communications running through a grid outage.

Food

33 acres of greenhouse · 600 acres of farm
  • Year-round greenhouses and the town farm supply most fresh vegetables, fruit and eggs. Neighboring ranches and orchards supply the rest.
  • A member-owned grocery sells at cost plus 8%.
  • Twelve neighborhood kitchens serve a $9 chef-cooked meal, every day.
  • The $400 food budget covers $220 of groceries and 20 kitchen meals.

Restaurants and main street

1.0 million sq ft · 40+ independent kitchens
  • A market hall with capped-rent stalls lets a cook open a restaurant for under $30,000.
  • Cafes, a brewery, bakeries and shops line the commons and the ground floors of the ring street.
  • Commercial rents are set as a share of sales in the first three years, so new businesses survive.

Homes and community living

– courtyard blocks · – homes each
  • Four and five-storey timber blocks around a shared garden, built from panels made in the town's own factory.
  • Private homes from studios to three-bedrooms, plus co-living suites.
  • Each block shares a guest suite, workshop, laundry, bike room and rooftop.
  • Each neighborhood has a house with a kitchen, child care, tool library and co-working floor.

Healthcare

$340 per adult · same-day primary care
  • A town health center with primary and urgent care, dental, mental health, a birth center, imaging and pharmacy.
  • Membership primary care at $85 a month with no co-pays.
  • A community health plan for hospital and specialist care, target $255 a month. Residents on Medicare or Medicaid pay less.
  • Inpatient care at the regional hospital 15 miles away.

Education

Birth to career · no tuition
  • Early-childhood centers in every neighborhood.
  • Four public K-12 campuses funded by state per-pupil dollars, with outdoor and project-based programs.
  • A learning campus for college courses, trades apprenticeships in the housing factory, and adult classes.
  • Library, maker labs and studios open to every resident.

Web access

1 Gb symmetric in every home · 10 Gb ready
  • Town-owned fiber to every room that needs it, included in the bill.
  • Free Wi-Fi across streets, parks and trailheads.
  • Two separate backbone routes and a satellite backup.
  • A bulk mobile plan at $15 a line.

Getting around

12-minute walk edge to center · no car needed
  • Electric shuttles circle the loop every four minutes, free.
  • E-bike share and a 400-car electric car-share fleet for trips out.
  • Private cars park at the arrival hub, not on streets.
  • Regional buses to the airport, Montrose, Ouray and Telluride.

Commons and outdoors

60 miles of trail from the front door
  • A 40-acre central commons with a pond, amphitheater and market hall.
  • Recreation center with pools, climbing wall and bath house.
  • River park with a whitewater course and swimming beach.
  • Trailheads, a gear library and a 300-bed lodge and hostel for visitors.
Investment case

An infrastructure-grade yield with land upside

Cairn is a long-life real asset: – rental homes, a utility, and a town center, on land bought at farm value. It suits infrastructure, pension, build-to-rent and impact capital. It is not a venture-return story, and the numbers below do not pretend otherwise.

Why it earns

  • Demand: half of US renters spend over 30% of income on housing. A complete life for $2,000 has few substitutes.
  • Integration: the operator earns on housing, energy, water, fiber and commercial space, so each can be priced near cost and the whole still yields –.
  • Cost: an on-site panel factory building one product – times targets $185 per sq ft against $250 or more for one-off projects.
  • Land: 2,400 acres at farm value become a town. That uplift stays in the asset.

What protects residents

  • A community land trust holds the ground. Investors own the buildings and the operating company.
  • Rents rise with inflation and no faster, written into the ground lease.
  • Residents elect a third of the operating board.
  • The operator is a public benefit corporation. Investor returns are real but capped by that charter.
